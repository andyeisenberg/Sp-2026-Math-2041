    Subsection Existence and Uniqueness

    Recall Theorem 7 from last time. Let’s see an example.
    🔗

    Example 8.

    Consider \(y' = xy^{3/2} = f(x, y)\text{.}\) Then \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{3}{2} xy^{1/2}\text{.}\) Both \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) are continuous when \(y \geq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The theorem clearly does not apply at the initial condition point \(c\text{.}\) At this point, \(y \\lt 0\text{,}\) so the functions \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) are not defined.
    🔗
    At \(a\text{,}\) \(y > 0\text{,}\) so both \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) exist. More importantly, we can draw a small circle around that point, and \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) are continuous in the entire circle (aka, "near" \(a\)). So the theorem applies at \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    At \(b\text{,}\) both \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) exist. However, any circle around \(b\) includes points where \(f, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) don’t exist. So the theorem doesn’t apply at \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Notation: for brevity, we can write \(f_y = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Separable Equations

    Definition 10.

    A DE is separable if it can be written in the form:
    \begin{gather*} \frac{dy}{dx} = y' = f(x)g(y) \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In this case:
    \begin{align*} h(y)\ dy = \frac{1}{g(y)}\ dy \amp = f(x)\ dx \\ \Rightarrow \quad \int h(y)\ dy \amp = \int f(x)\ dx \end{align*}
    Question: Is it okay to separate the differentials like this? To integrate each side?
    🔗
    Justification:
    \begin{align*} \frac{dy}{dx} \amp = f(x) \frac{1}{h(y)} \\ h(y) \frac{dy}{dx} \amp = f(x) \\ \int h(y) \frac{dy}{dx}\ dx \amp = \int f(x)\ dx \\ \int h(u)\ du \amp = \int f(x)\ dx \\ \int h(y)\ dy \amp = \int f(x)\ dx \end{align*}
    (Note the \(u\)-substitution \(u = y\) moving from line 3 to line 4.)
    🔗
    We don’t need to reshow this justification every time we separate variables. But now we see that it’s not shenanigans or a magic trick, it’s just the Chain Rule.
    🔗

    Example 11.

    Solve \(y' = \frac{x}{y}\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \int y\ dy \amp = \int x\ dx \\ \frac{y^2}{2} \amp \frac{x^2}{2} + C \\ y^2 \amp = x^2 + C. \end{align*}
    This is an implicit solution to the DE.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.

    Solve \(\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - 2xy^2 = f(x, y)\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{dy}{dx} \amp (1 - 2x)y^2 \\ \int y^{-2}\ dy \amp = \int 1 - 2x\ dx \\ \frac{-1}{y} \amp = x - x^2 + C \\ y \amp = \frac{1}{x^2 - x + C} \end{align*}
    However, moving from line 2 to line 3, we divided by \(y^2\text{.}\) This assumed that \(y \neq 0\text{.}\) Looking back at the DE, it seems that \(y = 0\) is a solution. So we must remember to include it when we write the general solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection First Order Linear

    Definition 13.

    A DE which can be put in the form
    \begin{align*} y' + p(x) y \amp f(x) \end{align*}
    is called a first order linear equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Idea: to solve these DEs, we notice that the LHS almost looks like a derivative taken via the Product Rule. Can we find a factor function \(r(x)\) to multiply both sides of the DE to get:
    \begin{align*} ry' + rpy \amp = ry' + r' y? \end{align*}
    If so, then:
    \begin{align*} ry' + rpy \amp = ry' + r' y \\ rpy \amp = r' y \\ rp \amp = r' \\ \int \frac{r'(x)}{r(x)}\ dx \amp = \int p(x)\ dx \\ \ln|r(x)| \amp = \int p(x)\ dx \\ r(x) \amp = e^{\int p(x)\ dx} \end{align*}
    This function is called an integrating factor.
    🔗

    Example 14.

    \begin{align*} v' \amp = 9.8 - \frac{v}{5} \\ v' + \frac{v}{5} \amp = 9.8 \\ r(t) \amp = e^{\int \frac{1}{5}\ dt} = e^{t/5} \\ \Rightarrow e^{t/5} v' + \frac{e^{t/5}}{5} v \amp = 9.8 e^{t/5} \\ \int \left(e^{t/5} v\right)'\ dt \amp = \int 9.8 e^{t/5}\ dt \\ e^{t/5} v \amp = 49 e^{t/5} + C \\ v \amp = 49 + C e^{-t/5} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 15.

    Solve \(y' = 2x(2 - y), y(0) = 5\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} y' \amp 4x - 2xy \\ y' + 2xy \amp = 4x \\ r(x) \amp = e^{\int p(x)\ dx} = e^{\int 2x\ dx} = e^{x^2} \\ \int \left(e^{x^2} y\right)'\ dx \amp = \int 4xe^{x^2}\ dx \\ e^{x^2} y \amp = 2e^{x^2} + C \\ y \amp = 2 + Ce^{-x^2} \end{align*}
    Applying the IC \(y(0) = 5\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 5 \amp = 2 + Ce^0 \\ C \amp = 3 \\ \Rightarrow y \amp = 2 + 3e^{-x^2} \end{align*}
    🔗
